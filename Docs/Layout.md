# Layout design

- `FBlockBoard` keeps the blocks: their order, their layout in rows and their lifetime, since removing a block destroys it. It works on its blocks itself, minimizing the propositions or returning the blocks' tokens and states, and never hands out its list (see Tell, Don't Ask in the Engineering principles of `CLAUDE.md`). `AAbstractBlocksManager`, the base of the formula and the palettes, holds one (see `Docs/BlocksManager.md`); the formula also keeps what its blocks mean (the selection, and later the tree). The board moves blocks only when the manager asks, and the manager's subclass decides what a gesture means (in the formula, a drag moves the block; in a palette, a tap inserts and nothing moves), so the formula and the palettes share the board (separation of mechanism and policy). As an object they contain, not a base they inherit, the board may offer what only some of them use, such as minimizing the propositions (see Engineering principles in `CLAUDE.md`). It is a `USTRUCT` held in a `UPROPERTY`, rather than a plain class, because the garbage collector sees the blocks only through reflected properties; it becomes a UObject in 1.24 (below).
- For now, `AAbstractBlocksManager` creates the blocks, registers them (their engine `Owner` and their events), listens to their events and asks the board for the work each gesture needs (see `Docs/BlocksManager.md`). The board cannot listen to them itself while it is a struct: the blocks' events are bound with `BindUObject`, which takes only engine objects, and `BindRaw`, for plain classes, would tie each block to the board's address in memory, so a copy of the board would leave the blocks bound to the old one.
- `Blocks` is the only source of the order. Rows and positions are always derived from it, never adjusted one block at a time.
- Rows break like the lines of a text (greedy line breaking): each block joins the last row if it fits within the maximum width, or else starts a new row, so no block passes the right edge. A row always starts with a block, so a block wider than the maximum stays alone in its row and is the one exception. Rows are recomputed from the order whenever it or a width changes, so blocks move between rows when a proposition is minimized or maximized, like words in a paragraph.
- The rows fill an area as wide as the maximum width, centered on the manager's position, and all start at its left edge, like a paragraph: a short formula is not centered, but nothing shifts sideways when the rows change during a drag, and in the formula the area is the formula's area of `Docs/Commands.md`. The first row passes through the manager's position and new rows appear below it, so a new row never moves the others. The board receives from the manager the maximum width, the blocks' height and the distance between rows, in block heights, which each subclass decides: 1.5 in the formula, which leaves half a block between rows, and 1.1 in the palettes (see `Docs/Scale.md` and `Docs/BlocksManager.md`). The three come together, once, through `SetData`, and the board derives from them how far apart its rows are and how high a dragged block rises.
- `FBlockRow` is a range of the board's list (its first and last index), with its width, the point where it starts and the maximum width; it keeps no blocks. Its methods that look at blocks receive the list as a `const` parameter, and changing the order stays in the board, whose data it is. It has no getters or setters: its constructor builds it with its first block, and it grows only through its own methods.
- Blocks enter through `AddBlock` (one, at the end), `AddBlockBefore` (one, before a given block, for inserting before the selected block; see `Docs/Commands.md`) or `AddBlocks` (several at once, such as the initial ones), and leave through `RemoveBlock` or `RemoveAllBlocks`, which destroy them. The manager registers each block (its engine `Owner` and its events) in the same request that hands it to the board. Every board method that changes the blocks leaves the rows laid out.
- While dragging, the block follows the pointer in both directions, held inside the formula's area: across, within the maximum width; down, between the first and the last row. A drop therefore always lands inside the area: dropping a block on a palette to remove it is only an idea (see Ideas in `CLAUDE.md`).
- The row under the dragged block's center decides where it goes. In its own row, it swaps with a neighbor when its edge facing that neighbor passes the neighbor's center (it covers half of it); only the neighbors in that row are checked, and only that row is laid out again after each swap, since a swap inside a row changes neither its blocks nor its width. In another row, it enters right before the first block of that row whose center is to the right of its own center, or at the row's end, and the rows are recomputed; if it does not fit there, it starts the next row, together with the blocks that followed it.
- This is stable: when the block does not fit and goes down, the blocks after it go down too, so every block left in the row under the pointer is to its left, and the next frame finds the same place, which changes nothing and recomputes nothing. In every case, the dragged block is put back under the cursor in the same frame.
- Planned (1.24): the board becomes a UObject and the blocks' facade (a single entry point for the classes that use them), so the formula and the palettes ask it for everything about their blocks. It keeps the factory and the sentences, builds the factory once it receives the blocks' height (see `Docs/Scale.md`), creates the blocks, registers their events with `BindUObject`, handles their width changes itself and relays to the manager the gestures whose meaning the subclasses decide. It also takes the requests that only the formula makes, now in the manager, such as minimizing or maximizing the selected proposition (see `Docs/BlocksManager.md`), and the casts to the concrete block classes, so that only it and the factory know them. Reasons: the board already keeps and destroys the blocks, so creating them there follows the Creator principle (GRASP: whoever aggregates the objects creates them); creating, registering and placing a block become one request to the class that keeps the blocks; and, as a facade, its interior can later be split into helpers that receive the list of blocks as a parameter, as `FBlockRow` does, without changing the classes that use it. Costs: the board needs the manager, which is the blocks' engine `Owner`, and the world to spawn them; relay code for the gestures; and, until the gestures change (1.25), it also relays the propositions' own long-press event.
- Rejected: attaching each block to its left neighbor (dragging would carry the blocks on its right, and the order would live in two places); swapping when the center passes the neighbor's center (a wide block cannot pass a narrow one at the end of a row) or the neighbor's edge (blocks of different widths swap back and forth every frame); the swap rule for entering another row (it compares the block with its neighbors in the order, and a block arriving from another row has none there yet); centering each row or the set of rows (the rows would shift sideways when they change during a drag); rows centered vertically on the formula (a new row would move the others); free dragging outside the formula's area (exceptions for drops outside it, which no command uses while dropping on a palette is only an idea); getters and setters for every field of `FBlockRow` (the same data, with more code); `FBlockRow` keeping a pointer to the list (a copy of the board would leave its rows pointing at the old list); a public `AddBlock` that leaves the layout to its caller (called alone, it leaves the block out of place); only `AddBlock`, called once per block (the initial blocks arrive together); `AddBlock` calling `AddBlocks` with a one-block list (harder to read than two methods that share private ones); the board handing out its list, through a getter or `begin`/`end` iterators (the formula worked on the board's data instead of asking the board to do the work); the formula destroying the blocks that the board removed (the board, which keeps them, ends their lifetime); the board as an actor (1.24 needs an engine object only to bind the blocks' events, and the board has no place in the world); the selection in the board (choosing and using the selected block is the formula's part, and keeping a reference to a block the board holds is fine); handing the selected block over to the formula until it is deselected (selecting points at a block without taking it off the board); the distance between rows as a constant of the board (the palettes' rows are closer than the formula's); separate setters for the distance between rows, the blocks' height and the maximum width (the three come together, once, and had to be called in a fixed order, since the board computed the rows' distance when it got the height).
