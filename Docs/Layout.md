# Layout design

- `UBlockBoard` is the blocks' facade, the single entry point for the classes that use them: it creates the blocks, keeps them in order, registers their events and ends their lifetime, since removing a block destroys it. It works on its blocks itself, minimizing the propositions or returning the blocks' tokens and states, and never hands out its list (see Tell, Don't Ask in the Engineering principles of `CLAUDE.md`). Creating the blocks where they are kept follows the Creator principle (GRASP: whoever aggregates the objects creates them) and makes creating, registering and placing a block a single request (see `Docs/BlockCreation.md`). `AAbstractBlocksManager`, the base of the formula and the palettes, holds the board as a protected attribute, which its subclasses use directly (see `Docs/BlocksManager.md`); the formula also keeps what its blocks mean (the selection, and later the tree).
- The board moves blocks only when its holder asks, and the holder decides what a block's gesture means (in the formula, a drag moves the block; in a palette, a tap inserts and nothing moves), so the formula and the palettes share the board (separation of mechanism and policy). As an object they contain, not a base they inherit, the board may offer what only some of them use, such as minimizing the propositions (see Engineering principles in `CLAUDE.md`).
- The board is a `UObject`, created in the manager's constructor with `CreateDefaultSubobject`, so the manager is its Outer, the object it lives in. It reaches the world through `GetWorld`, which a `UObject` asks of its Outer, and gives each block the manager as its engine `Owner`, found with `GetTypedOuter<AActor>`, without knowing the manager's class. It binds the blocks' events with `BindUObject`, which takes only engine objects: it handles their width changes itself, laying the rows out again, and relays their gestures to its holder (tapped, long pressed, drag started, dragged and drag ended), which decides what they mean (see `Docs/BlocksManager.md`). Its events carry an `ABlock`, so only the board and the factory know the concrete block classes (see Engineering principles in `CLAUDE.md`).
- `Blocks` is the only source of the order. Rows and positions are always derived from it, never adjusted one block at a time.
- `FBlockBoardRows`, a plain class the board holds as a member, keeps the rows and the area they fill: its origin, the maximum width, the distance between rows and how high a dragged block rises. It lays the blocks out and handles dragging, including the order changes a drag makes. The board decides which blocks exist (it alone adds them to its list and removes them), and the rows class decides where they stand (it only reorders them). Like `FBlockRow`, it receives the board's list as a parameter, `const` where it only reads it, and keeps no blocks, so the garbage collector keeps seeing them through the board's `UPROPERTY`. Its methods that use the area but not the rows (the edges, the position of each row, the row under a point, the lifted position of a dragged block) stay with it, since they use its data, and laying out the rows needs them too.
- Rows break like the lines of a text (greedy line breaking): each block joins the last row if it fits within the maximum width, or else starts a new row, so no block passes the right edge. A row always starts with a block, so a block wider than the maximum stays alone in its row and is the one exception. Rows are recomputed from the order whenever it or a width changes, so blocks move between rows when a proposition is minimized or maximized, like words in a paragraph.
- The rows fill an area as wide as the maximum width, centered on the manager's position, and all start at its left edge, like a paragraph: a short formula is not centered, but nothing shifts sideways when the rows change during a drag, and in the formula the area is the formula's area of `Docs/Commands.md`. The first row passes through the manager's position and new rows appear below it, so a new row never moves the others. The board receives from the manager the maximum width, the blocks' height and the distance between rows, in block heights, which each subclass decides: 1.5 in the formula, which leaves half a block between rows, and 1.1 in the palettes (see `Docs/Scale.md` and `Docs/BlocksManager.md`). The three come together, once, through `SetData`; the board passes them on to its rows, which derive from them how far apart the rows are and how high a dragged block rises, and builds its factory with the height.
- `FBlockRow` is a range of the board's list (its first and last index), with its width, the point where it starts and the maximum width; it keeps no blocks. Its methods that look at blocks receive the list as a `const` parameter, and changing the order stays in `FBlockBoardRows`. It has no getters or setters: its constructor builds it with its first block, and it grows only through its own methods.
- Blocks enter through `CreateBlocks` (several at once, such as the initial ones) or through a request for one block: `CreateBlock` (at the end, for a tap on a palette item with nothing selected), `CreateBlockBefore` (before a given block, for a tap with a block selected), `CreateBlockAfter` (after a given block, for a long press with a block selected) and `CreateBlockAtStart` (at the start, for a long press with nothing selected; see `Docs/Commands.md`). Each is a single request that creates, registers and places the blocks; the requests for one block differ only in where it enters the list, so they share one private method that takes that position. Blocks leave through `RemoveBlock` or `RemoveAllBlocks`, which destroy them. Every board method that changes the blocks leaves the rows laid out.
- While dragging, the block follows the pointer in both directions, held inside the formula's area: across, within the maximum width; down, between the first and the last row. A drop therefore always lands inside the area: dropping a block on a palette to remove it is only an idea (see Ideas in `CLAUDE.md`).
- The row under the dragged block's center decides where it goes. In its own row, it swaps with a neighbor when its edge facing that neighbor passes the neighbor's center (it covers half of it); only the neighbors in that row are checked, and only that row is laid out again after each swap, since a swap inside a row changes neither its blocks nor its width. In another row, it enters right before the first block of that row whose center is to the right of its own center, or at the row's end, and the rows are recomputed; if it does not fit there, it starts the next row, together with the blocks that followed it.
- This is stable: when the block does not fit and goes down, the blocks after it go down too, so every block left in the row under the pointer is to its left, and the next frame finds the same place, which changes nothing and recomputes nothing. In every case, the dragged block is put back under the cursor in the same frame.
- Rejected: attaching each block to its left neighbor (dragging would carry the blocks on its right, and the order would live in two places); swapping when the center passes the neighbor's center (a wide block cannot pass a narrow one at the end of a row) or the neighbor's edge (blocks of different widths swap back and forth every frame); the swap rule for entering another row (it compares the block with its neighbors in the order, and a block arriving from another row has none there yet); centering each row or the set of rows (the rows would shift sideways when they change during a drag); rows centered vertically on the formula (a new row would move the others); free dragging outside the formula's area (exceptions for drops outside it, which no command uses while dropping on a palette is only an idea); getters and setters for every field of `FBlockRow` (the same data, with more code); `FBlockRow` or `FBlockBoardRows` keeping a pointer to the list (the list is the board's, and a stored pointer would point at the wrong list if its holder were copied); a public request that adds a block and leaves the layout to its caller (called alone, it leaves the block out of place); a single request for one block, called once per block (the initial blocks arrive together); the request for one block calling the one for several with a one-block list (harder to read than two methods that share private ones); the board handing out its list, through a getter or `begin`/`end` iterators (the formula worked on the board's data instead of asking the board to do the work); the formula destroying the blocks that the board removed (the board, which keeps them, ends their lifetime); the board as a struct, with the manager creating the blocks and listening to them (a struct cannot bind the blocks' events: `BindUObject` takes only engine objects, and `BindRaw` would tie each block to the board's address, so a copy of the board would leave the blocks bound to the old one; and creating, registering and placing a block were split between two classes); the board as an actor (it needs to be an engine object only to bind the blocks' events, and it has no place in the world); `FBlockBoardRows` only laying out the rows and answering questions, with dragging in the board (the drag would be split between two classes, and the board would ask the rows class for its rows to work on them, against Tell, Don't Ask); the area's geometry (its edges, the position of each row, the row under a point, the dragged block's height) in the board (laying out the rows needs it, so the board would keep a copy or the rows class would ask for it); the selection in the board (choosing and using the selected block is the formula's part, and keeping a reference to a block the board holds is fine); handing the selected block over to the formula until it is deselected (selecting points at a block without taking it off the board); the distance between rows as a constant of the board (the palettes' rows are closer than the formula's); separate setters for the distance between rows, the blocks' height and the maximum width (the three come together, once, and had to be called in a fixed order, since the board computed the rows' distance when it got the height).
